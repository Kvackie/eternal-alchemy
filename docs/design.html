Game design document  ·  draft 8  ·  as built after bottling became one press

Eternal Alchemy
a potion shop, kept slowly

A cozy single-player alchemy shop for mobile and desktop. Grow it in the garden, raise it in the cave, cut it from the shaft, or send someone braver to fetch it — then match essences in the cauldron, bottle it, and price it right. No debt, no deadline, no gems.

Changelog

What changed

Your answers, applied. Further down, new marks something added after the first draft, and rebuilt marks a section rewritten for draft 7 or 8.

Draft 8 — bottling is one press

Vessels and seals were a second set of choices asked of every finished brew, and they were not part of the design the game is being built to now. Playing it made the case: most vessels and seals were sold by no merchant at all — the Crystal Orb, the only vessel that would hold a Sovereign brew, among them — and the supply of glass bottlenecked the whole economy. This draft takes them out and records what else moved with them.

No vessels, no seals

When a brew finishes you press Bottle it — on the bench's Ready card, on the pot, or in the station — and it is bottled. No dialog, no choice, nothing consumed. A potion's value is its recipe's base × potency × grade, and every potency can be bottled, Sovereign included. Bottling.

What went with them

Shelf appeal from glass and wax, the Ashwalker's mark, hero supplies a grade better than they were, the guild stamp's contract bonus, the amphora's three units, the pouch's stack of five, and the volatile trait. A bottle is one unit and fills one shelf slot.

Two pieces of equipment that did nothing

The vessel moulds and the sealing press were on sale for 900g and 1200g and never did anything in the code. They are gone, and the equipment list is thirteen pieces.

Every save carried forward

Migration v18 drops vessel and seal stock, strips both from every stored bottle and prices it again, drops them from every order, hands back the extra bottles on a stacked shelf, deals the stalls again, refunds the moulds and the press, and clears log lines naming what was removed.

The S bar rises with the mix

S is from 90 purity for one- or two-essence potions, 94 for three and 97 for four or five, and every band above F moves up with it. One ladder for every potion made a five-essence S as easy as a single-essence one. Grades.

No order shown twice

The board never holds two orders asking one faction for the same number of the same potion at the same grade. Orders that are merely close still appear.

Draft 7 — the potion overhaul

The game had grown a second and third test on every brew — a temperature band and a method — and a recipe book of 196 that held together only because of them: most of its recipes were twins told apart by method or by an essence window. Under it sat 385 ingredients, most of them near-duplicates at strengths nobody chose, so balancing a ratio was luck rather than arithmetic. This draft cuts the brew back to the blend, rebuilds the book and the ingredients around that, and records what went.

Brewing is mixing

No heat, no temperature, no method. Put ingredients in the pot, and nothing is spent; read the outcome, then Accept, which spends them and starts the brew timer, or Pour it back, which returns everything. Bottle when the timer finishes. Brewing.

31 potions, one per set of essences

One recipe for every non-empty set of the five essences, always in equal measure. Each cone is half the angle to its nearest neighbour, so cones never overlap and no blend has two answers. A blend inside no cone makes nothing and cannot be accepted, which is why Murk is gone.

Grade from the ratio, potency from the size

Purity is 100 on the exact ratio and falls in a straight line to 0 at the cone's edge; the letter is read straight off it, S from 90 for the simple ones and higher for bigger mixes. Potency is total essence, in tiers that match the pots' capacities. A small pot on the ratio is an S; a Sovereign off it is an F.

151 ingredients on one scale

Every amount of essence is one of 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24, 32, 36 or 48, and each essence has a clean ingredient at every step. The mixes are built from the same numbers. Drying weakens an ingredient to 0.9× and then 0.8×, which is what throws a ratio out. Essences.

Each kind comes from one place

Every herb grows in the garden, every fungus spreads in the cave, every mineral is somewhere down the quarry — a new stratum every five metres — and exotics are found on expeditions. scripts/gen-ingredients.js holds those rules, and a data test fails when the data drifts from them. Sources.

One trade per merchant

Bramm the herb seeds, Vessa the spores and the upper quarry, Hesk the lower quarry, the Ashwalker the exotics. Trade goods are dealt in turn, so anything a merchant sells turns up within a known number of visits. Merchants.

Cut, and why

Bottle forms asked a second, unrelated set of questions of a finished brew. The greenhouse bred strains whose essence drifted off every clean number. The Lagged cauldron and the Codex's Deft Hands only acted on heat. The reagent category is folded into exotics.

Every save carried forward

Four migrations, v14 to v17. Old recipes fold into the potion of the same essences, removed ingredients into the nearest kept one of the same kind, and what was bought and removed is paid back: Lagged 180g, the greenhouse 1400g, Deft Hands its Mastery.

The art pipeline stepped back

Colour no longer decides essence; the slot on the scale does. The scripts that turned sprites into ingredients, matched bottles to recipes and generated the old book are gone. Every picture was kept. Art.

What is still placeholder

The 31 potions are named for their essences ("Aqua–Terra Potion"), priced by how many they use (12, 20, 30, 42 and 56 gold base), and hinted in the book only by that count. Each borrows an existing painted bottle. Merchant prices, grow times, cave rates and barter costs are worked out from each ingredient's strength rather than tuned. All of it is scaffolding for a pass that has not happened yet.

Draft 6 — the art arrived, and the game grew to fit it

Roughly six hundred painted sprites landed at once: herbs, fungi, gems, bottles, furnishings and character portraits. Absorbing them turned out to be a content problem rather than an art problem, and the answer was to stop hand-authoring and let the pictures decide.

Essence is read off the sprite

The UI already tints and labels everything by dominant essence, so art that disagrees with its own vector teaches the wrong thing at a glance. Each sprite's colour now derives its essence: a red leaf is Ignis because it is red. Three hundred ingredients, none of them hand-typed. How it works.

314 ingredients, 71 recipes

Up from 18 and 16. Every one has a source — a crop, a cave species, a shaft seam, a biome's loot table — because an ingredient nobody can gather is the bug this project keeps re-learning. The recipe book grew until the cone geometry ran out of room.

Gems gave the shaft a reason

161 crystals became minerals: stable, dense, and the precise instrument for hitting a ratio herbs can only approximate. They also fixed a real weakness — the herb art was overwhelmingly green and red, and Aer and Umbra were barely supplied until the gems arrived in every colour.

Faces, bottles and furnishings

Merchants and heroes have portraits. Each recipe has its own painted bottle, matched by the colour of the liquid rather than the glass. Décor was rebuilt around what the art actually is — seven banners for the wall, chests and coin for the floor.

An atlas was the wrong answer

Named as the fix, built, measured, and then not shipped: 2.8 MB fetched whole against 135 KB for the sixteen sprites any screen actually shows. Atlases win when a screen draws most of a set at once, and none here does. The numbers.

A data file was lost

A crashed generator truncated heroes.json to nothing, and this repository has no version control to restore from. Rebuilt by hand; every generator now writes through a temp file and renames, so a crash can no longer destroy what it was rewriting.

Draft 5 — M4, and what an audit turned up

The content set is in and the systems the last draft called unbuilt are built. Then a deliberate sweep for declared-but-unwired data found that a good deal of what this document promised was never reachable in play — the most useful thing the project has done for itself.

Discovery and hidden bands shipped

Draft 4 called this "the most promising unbuilt idea in the document". It is now the answer to what the alembic wheel took away: a recipe you have not made does not tell you its temperature, and you bisect toward it by brewing. Only accepting teaches, so free rejection stays free. Brewing.

Ten upgrades nobody could buy

Over half the equipment tree was in no merchant's stock, and a purchase is the only way equipment is ever granted. The support beams were among them, so the shaft could never leave its first seam — and the greenhouse, so crossbreeding was dead content. A fourth merchant, Hesk the outfitter, now sells the infrastructure.

Seven of eight town modifiers did nothing

Prestige exists so a second run plays differently, and the function returning a town's modifiers was exported and never called once. Choosing a town was very nearly cosmetic. All seven are wired: crop time, ore richness, prices, cave spread, beds, contract payout, footfall. The long distillation.

Décor is placed, not owned

The shop's furnishings moved out of the equipment tree, where owning one was the whole decision. Ten pieces, five spots — so a full collection still leaves you choosing which five are out, and the window is a real trade between day appeal and night trade.

A recipe has to be brewable

The book roughly doubled to sixteen. The old check asked whether some ingredient pointed within 45° of a recipe; the real question is whether a blend can land inside its cone, and three candidates passed the weak test while being impossible. There is no pure-fire recipe because every fire source in the game drags Terra along with it.

The harness now asks the flat question

Five separate times, a data file declared something and the far end was never wired — equipment, half the Codex, five vessel and seal fields, the soil on every crop, the town modifiers. Every one passed the suite. A coverage test now fails when any field in any data file is read by nothing.

Draft 4 — brought in line with what shipped

M1, M2 and M3 are built and playable. This draft corrects the document where the code took a different, better road — and marks what the building taught.

The alembic wheel was cut

It read as "a spinning thing" and asked for timing the game never otherwise asks for. Replaced by three legible steps: ingredients, temperature and method, accept or reject. The full account, including what that cost.

Merchants got a screen

Planned as an overlay. Four traders on separate cycles plus the upgrade trees needed more surface than a banner. The Workbench went the other way and folded into the Cauldron.

Nothing is scheduled

Merchant visits, their stock, cave spread and walk-in customers are all derived from the day counter. Offline catch-up needs no special handling, and a reload cannot reshuffle a shelf.

Ten save migrations so far

Every reshaping of the world has been a versioned step that carries an existing shop forward. A save from the alembic era still loads.

Draft 3 — the last four calls

The Ashwalker walks at night

The one merchant exception. Bramm and Vessa keep daylight hours; the Umbra trader appears only after dark, which gives night a trading phase of its own.

Day length stays tunable

3 real hours is the shipping default, but it lives in one config value and a debug time panel lands in M1 — you cannot develop an offline-progress game while waiting 18 real hours for a crop.

The wheel grows with the alembic

Actions scale 3 → 7 across five bought alembic tiers, plus slot-in modules. Early brews stay teachable; late brews get the full triangle.

Customers show their stance

Three portrait poses per customer carry the haggle stance; dialogue carries personality. ~60 portraits for a 20-customer roster — budgeted deliberately.

Draft 2 — the big additions

Named & scoped

Eternal Alchemy — the prestige system makes the name literal. Web and mobile only, no Steam. Local saves. No audio in scope; localisation and accessibility are in from day one.

Two new sources

A mushroom cave and a mining shaft join the garden — each with its own verb, not three timer farms with different art.

The wheel got a triangle

Heat and Chill join Stir. The wheel now steers both grading axes: angle is purity, radius is potency. Prototype below is updated.

Haggling is now rock-paper-scissors

Customers hold a telegraphed stance; your pitch actions counter one and backfire against another. Countering forces them to switch, so it's a moving read.

Renown gates, gold buys

Upgrades are no longer free rank rewards. Rank makes equipment appear in a merchant's stock; you still pay for it. Every system got a buyable equipment tree.

More to make, more to make it in

10 forms, 8 vessels, 7 seals. Plus a day/night cycle with merchants closed at night, hero favour, and a prestige reset with a permanent Mastery tree.

Direction

Four pillars

Everything below should trace back to one of these. If a feature doesn't, it's cut.

Pillar 01 — Craft, not clicks

Every potion is the result of decisions you made: which ingredients, in what ratio, how fresh they were, how much of it you made. Two players' Aqua–Terra potions should differ.

Pillar 02 — Time is a rhythm, not a wall

Growth and travel take real hours because that gives the shop a heartbeat and makes returning feel like arriving. Nothing is gated behind waiting you can pay to skip — there is nothing to pay with.

Pillar 03 — Three ways to sell, three moods

The shelf is passive income you tune. Contracts are planning. Face-to-face haggling is the one place the game asks for your full attention.

Pillar 04 — Nothing is lost

No spoilage to zero, no failed harvests, no dead heroes, no debt collector. Setbacks cost renown or gold, never progress. This is the promise that lets someone put it down for a week.

Structure

The core loop

Four sources funnel into one cauldron. Two feedback lines close the system: gold buys stock and equipment, and your own potions supply the heroes who fetch what you can't grow.

The dashed lines are the ones that matter. Gold buys equipment, so income is always convertible into capability rather than just accumulating. And heroes consume your own potions, which turns your product into an input, gives you a reason to brew things you'd never sell, and stops the mid-game from becoming a pure export economy.

System

Time, the day cycle & offline progress

One authoritative world clock in milliseconds. Everything that takes time is a scheduled job against it, so a session resume and a live tick run identical code.

The day new

An in-game day is 3 real hours, not 24 — this is the decision that makes a day cycle safe. If the game's night were the real world's night, anyone who plays at 11pm would never see a merchant. At three hours, night is a passing weather condition, not a lockout:

ONE DAY = 3h real 0:00 Dawn 15 min · merchants opening, warm low light 0:15 Day 1h 45m · full trade, peak footfall 2:00 Dusk 15 min · merchants packing up, last calls 2:15 Night 45 min · merchants gone, Umbra ripens ONE WEEK = 7 days = 21h real weekday customer mix rotates

Lighting shifts continuously through the four phases — the shop's window, the garden's sky, the cave's lantern glow. It is cosmetic plus one rule: merchants are unavailable at night. Everything else — garden, cave, mine, cauldron, bottling, shelf, contracts, heroes — runs around the clock.

Night is content, not downtime

Because merchants close, night needs its own reasons to be there:

  • Moonlit crops. A handful of Umbra and Aer ingredients only ripen during night and must be harvested before dawn or they revert to a lesser stage — the one time-pressure element in the game, and it's opt-in.
  • The cave doesn't care. Mushrooms spread faster in darkness; night is the cave's peak hours.
  • Different customers. Night walk-ins are a distinct archetype set — nervous buyers, off-duty guards, people who'd rather not be seen. Lower footfall, higher margins.
  • Weekday flavour. Market Day (day 3) doubles footfall; Temple Day (day 7) brings about 15% more than an ordinary day.

The Ashwalker is the exception new

Bramm, Vessa and Hesk keep daylight hours. The Ashwalker only appears at night — the trader who deals in potions rather than gold is a worse character with a market stall and a sunny disposition. He is the reason to check the shop after dark, and the rule stays simple to explain because he is the only exception:

Bramm day every 2 days every herb's seed Vessa day every 5 days every spore, the upper quarry's minerals Hesk day every 3 days the lower quarry's minerals, the tools Ashwalker night every 8 days exotics, for potions only

Developing against a real clock new

A game where a mission takes hours and a contract runs days of play cannot be tested by waiting. A debug time panel ships in M1, not as an afterthought — it's the difference between verifying offline behaviour in seconds and verifying it never:

  • Time scale — ×1, ×10, ×60, ×600, and pause
  • Jump to phase — next dawn, dusk or night; skip n in-game days
  • Simulate absence — "I was away 72 hours", running the real resume path so the While-You-Were-Away panel can be tested without leaving
  • Finish all timers — every crop, vein, spread tile and mission completes now
  • Grant — gold, renown, rank, ingredients, recipes, equipment
  • Seed control — fix the RNG seed so a bug reproduces exactly

Dev builds show it as a panel; release builds keep it behind a hidden gesture for QA and strip it from the production bundle. Because every duration in the game is a config value rather than a constant, the panel is also where day length gets tuned — 3 hours is the shipping default, not a decision that's hard to revisit.

Offline rules

  • No offline cap. Close it for three weeks, come back, every plot is harvest-ready and every hero is home.
  • Shelf sales accrue offline at 40% footfall. Being present is rewarded by degree, never by gate.
  • The day cycle runs while you're away — it's derived from world time, so you might return at night. Contracts and merchant visits do not expire while closed: a contract's clock pauses and caravans queue rather than pass by.
  • Clock protection: we store lastSeenRealTime and only ever advance by a non-negative delta. Beyond that, in a premium single-player game with no leaderboards, we let cheaters cheat.
  • Return summary. One "While you were away" panel on resume — harvests, heroes, gold, contracts due. One tap to collect all.

Sources

Garden, cave & shaft rebuilt

Three sites you own. The design rule: each gets a different verb, or they're three timer farms wearing different art. Since draft 7 each also has exactly one kind of ingredient, and holds every one of that kind.

Garden — you plant

Verb: cultivate. Deliberate, predictable, per-plot. You choose exactly what grows and when. Yields herbs — the weakest ingredients, 4 to 12 essence a unit, renewable, and they age.

Cave — it spreads

Verb: tend. You seed a substrate and mycelium spreads across a grid on its own. Low-touch, emergent, occasionally surprising. Yields fungi — 12 to 18 essence a unit, and they age twice as fast as a herb.

Shaft — you dig

Verb: extract. Veins deplete and you go deeper. Equipment-hungry, front-loaded cost, high reward. Yields minerals — 24 to 36 essence a unit, stable forever, the densest thing you can gather yourself.

The fourth source is the heroes, and the fourth kind is theirs: exotics, 40 to 48 essence a unit, which never age and come only from expeditions or, dearly, from the Ashwalker. See Heroes.

The garden

Start with 4 outdoor plots. Each holds one crop and has a soil type — Loam, Ash or Silt — and a crop planted in the ground it wants yields one extra unit. A bonus rather than a penalty, deliberately: the wrong plot is a missed opportunity, never a punishment, and picking up a seed highlights the plot that suits it. The soil a crop wants follows its dominant essence — Ash for Ignis and Umbra, Silt for Aqua and Aer, Loam for Terra — so the rule can be learned rather than looked up.

60 crops, because every herb grows and nothing else does. Grow time is two and a half minutes per point of essence, so a weak clean herb takes ten minutes and a strong mix half an hour. Seeds come from Bramm, who carries every herb's seed, from expeditions, and back from the harvest itself. A new shop starts with four seeds each of five weak clean herbs, one per essence — Curlflame, Bluepetal, Duskberry, Featherfern and Pomegranate — which is exactly enough to make the five potions it already knows. Four plots means the garden is always a choice about what is worth the ground.

Nothing needs tending. A crop finishes on schedule at full yield whether or not anyone looks at it. Nothing wilts, nothing dies. That's Pillar 04, and it's what makes the garden safe to leave.

The greenhouse and crossbreeding were removed in draft 7. Bred strains carried essence amounts that drifted off every clean number, which is exactly what the one-scale ingredient set exists to prevent. Old saves keep the two beds the greenhouse added, fold every strain back into the crop it came from, and are paid back its 1400g.

The mushroom cave

A dark 6×6 grid beneath the shop. You don't plant tiles — you place a spore cluster and it spreads to adjacent tiles over real time. Each species spreads at its own rate and competes for tiles, so an unattended cave slowly converges on whatever grows fastest. Harvesting a tile clears it and opens space.

Three levers:

  • The humidity ward — an equipment purchase that raises the spread rate everywhere.
  • Lanterns — light favours Chalkgill and suppresses Gravecrown; darkness does the reverse. A species out of its preferred light still grows, but spreads at a quarter of the rate. Placing light is how you shape which mushrooms win, and it is a free tool rather than a purchase.
  • Substrate trays — pin a tile to one species so spreading neighbours can't take it, trading flexibility for reliability.

30 species, because every fungus is one. Their spores come from Vessa, who carries every species in turn, and from expeditions. A new shop starts with two clusters of Azurecap. Most species want light, three want the dark and eight do not mind, so a lantern is a real instrument rather than a decoration.

Spread is random, but not free-running. Each tick draws from its own stream, seeded by a seed the world keeps, so a night of spread plays out the same whether anyone watched it or it was caught up on return. Before draft 7 the cave shared the world's stream with the market, which spends it differently live and offline, and the same night grew a different cave depending on whether the game was open.

The cave rewards checking in without demanding it: leave it a week and you have a full harvest of something, just not necessarily what you wanted. That's a good failure — Pillar 04 holds, because a cave full of the wrong mushroom is still a cave full of mushrooms.

The mining shaft

A vertical cross-section, Potion-Craft-ish in feel: strata of rock with visible ore veins. You assign the shaft a target vein and it produces ore in batches over real time until the vein depletes. Then you dig deeper.

SHAFT depth 40m · supports rated to 60m Working Chalk Glass TER 32 · next batch 3m · 34% depleted Available Brine Glass AQU 16 · TER 16 Bark Opal IGN 8 · TER 8 · AER 8 · UMB 8 Deeper — 45m: Clay Shard (TER 16 · UMB 16) —

Every mineral is somewhere down it. A new stratum starts every five metres, forty in all from the surface to 195m, weakest at the top and strongest at the bottom. Each stratum's own mineral is the commonest vein in it, and the two minerals above it still turn up beside it, so a new depth never strands the one you were working. A depleted vein slowly replenishes while you work another, so a shallow seam never becomes dead content.

Depth is the progression axis, and it's bought, not unlocked. The shaft opens at the surface with the first 20m free to dig; each set of support beams from Hesk adds 20m more, up to ten sets. The steel pick adds an ore to every batch.

Minerals, and why the shaft matters rebuilt

Forty minerals, and they are not simply more ingredients. Minerals are stable — no freshness, no decay — and carry on average more than three times the essence of a herb per unit. That is the trade the whole gathering design rests on: herbs are cheap and perishable, stone is dearer and eternal, and a pot holds only so many ingredients, so filling a large one takes stone. The clean minerals sit at 24, 32 and 36; the rest are even pairs (16 + 16), lopsided pairs (24 + 12) and fours (8 of each).

Every essence has a clean mineral at each of those three strengths, and the quarry is laid out from that rule rather than from the art. Before draft 7 the gems came in by colour, a gem seam could be placed so that it silently shadowed a hand-authored stratum, and one did — nullstone stopped existing at 80m and took two recipes with it. The strata are now regular, generated steps, so there is nothing to shadow.

Minerals are not the densest ingredients in the game: exotics are, at 40 to 48 a unit. They are the densest you can dig for yourself, which is why the shaft is where a Grand or Sovereign brew usually starts.

Source

Merchants, stock & equipment rebuilt

Costs gold, arrives on a schedule, daytime only — and now the only place equipment comes from.

Four named traders, each on their own cycle, each with a personality and one trade. When one arrives you get a persistent banner (never a modal, never a timed pop-up) and their stall stays open until they leave. Merchants are unavailable at night — a daytime caravan that would have arrived after dark arrives at the following dawn instead — with one exception, below.

Bramm · day · every 2 days · seeds

Every herb's seed, sixty of them, plus the cheap furnishings, extra plots and shelves. Cheap, always useful, never exciting. The reliable floor.

Vessa · day · every 5 days · spores & stone

Every cave species' spores, and the minerals of the quarry's upper half for anyone who has not dug that far. The better furnishings and the wide rim beside them.

Hesk · day · every 3 days · infrastructure new

The outfitter. Beams, beds, the steel pick, bunk rooms — everything that makes the shop bigger rather than fuller — and the strong minerals of the quarry's lower half, for a shop that wants them before it has dug that deep. Slow and dear.

The Ashwalker · night · every 8 days · exotics

Doesn't take gold. Trades only for potions of a minimum grade, spending your cheapest qualifying bottles first, and is the only merchant who sells exotics. The rarest wait on a long acquaintance with him, so an expedition stays the cheaper way to them. The main sink that isn't money, and the only merchant you'll ever meet after dark.

Between them the four carry every ingredient in the game: a herb by its seed, a fungus by its spores, the rest as themselves. A stall is eight or nine things, in two parts. Trade goods are dealt in turn: each visit picks up round the merchant's list where the last visit stopped, so anything they sell turns up within a known number of visits. A weighted draw over sixty seeds could leave one unseen for a season, which is not acceptable when the seed is the only way to grow that herb. Staples — boards, furnishings, equipment — are still drawn at random, as they were before draft 7; they were not redistributed by trade.

Both parts come from the visit number and a seed derived from (merchant, day), so a stall is stable across saves, reloads and re-renders without a single item being stored. What you have already bought is the only state — recorded against the item rather than its position in the list, because a positional key silently re-maps onto different goods the moment a merchant's pool is edited, and marked things sold out that nobody had bought.

Every price also carries the town's price level: Hollowreach trades 10% under, Highmarch 35% over. The prices underneath are placeholders for now, worked out from each ingredient's strength rather than tuned one by one.

Equipment is bought, not awarded new

This is the draft-2 change with the widest reach. Previously renown ranks handed you upgrades. Now renown makes equipment appear in a merchant's stock, and gold buys it. Rank is permission; gold is acquisition. Every system has a tree:

TreeSold byWhat you buy

Thirteen pieces in all. The pots themselves are a ladder of their own, bought from the Cauldron screen: six sizes from 60 to 600 essence, and up to six pots at once. The vessel moulds and the sealing press left the tree in draft 8 — they never did anything, and old saves were paid back 900g and 1200g — the greenhouse and the Lagged cauldron in draft 7, and the order book before it.

This solves a real problem with the draft-1 economy: gold had too few sinks late, and rank-ups dumped capability all at once. Now every gold pile has an obvious next purchase and rank-ups feel like a shop restock rather than a gift.

Relationship levels rise as you trade, unlocking deeper stock tiers and a standing discount. They never decay. Standing orders, which had a merchant deliver a fixed basket every visit, were removed along with the order book that unlocked them; dealing trade goods in turn does much of the same job, since what you need is never more than a few visits away.

What changed, and why

At draft 5, ten of the nineteen pieces then on the tree were in no merchant's stock, and a purchase is the only way equipment is ever granted — so over half the tree was unobtainable while looking complete. The support beams were among them, and without beams the shaft could never leave its first seam, which stranded three of the four ores and two recipes behind them. The greenhouse was among them, so crossbreeding was dead content. So was the order book, so standing orders were unreachable.

Hesk exists because of that. It also answers a thinness the document had not admitted: three merchants is not many for an endless game, and a fourth whose whole identity is infrastructure gives the upgrade tree a home instead of crowding the staples out of Bramm's draw.

A second bug hid underneath: the stock filter hid anything already owned once, ignoring the declared repeat limit. Three tiers of support beams were one tier. Both are now covered — a test asserts every upgrade has a seller, and another that a repeatable one keeps being offered until its limit.

Craft — the heart

Essences & the cauldron rebuilt

Five essences, a capacity budget, and a target ratio. Get the ratio right and you've made the potion; get it exactly right and you've made a good one.

The five essences

Each essence has a colour and a glyph, used together everywhere in the UI — see accessibility:

IGN Ignis · heat, force, offence AQU Aqua · flow, healing, cleansing TER Terra · body, endurance, wards AER Aer · speed, mind, perception UMB Umbra · change, toxin, illusion

Every ingredient is a vector across those five, and nothing else about it matters mechanically — that's what keeps 151 ingredients authorable and every recipe provably brewable. The one trait there was, volatile, only decided which vessel a brew needed, and went with the vessels in draft 8.

One scale for every amount new

Every amount of essence in every ingredient is one of ten numbers: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24, 32, 36 and 48. Each essence has a clean ingredient — that essence and nothing else — at every step of the scale, and the mixes are built from the same numbers. The reason is arithmetic. With 385 ingredients at whatever strength a colour and a tier happened to produce, most of them near-duplicates, balancing a ratio was a matter of luck. On one scale it is sums a player can do, and a clean ingredient at every strength is what makes a lopsided correction possible at all: a blend can only reach the hull of what you hold.

Freshness throws a ratio out

An ingredient is at its stated strength while dewfresh, 0.9× once fresh, and 0.8× when dried. Fungi age twice as fast as herbs; minerals and exotics never age. The point is not that old stock is weaker — it is that one dried ingredient in a pot of fresh ones shifts the ratio, so the pot you balanced yesterday is off today. The drying rack still exists: drying on demand makes an ingredient weaker on purpose, which is one more strength to balance with.

How a brew resolves

  1. Fill the cauldron. Drop in ingredients; their essence vectors sum. Each pot has a capacity — 60 essence for the first, up to 600 for the largest — and a limit on how many ingredients it takes. Exceed the capacity and the brew is Unstable: still made, but capped at grade D.
  2. Identify the potion. The summed vector is a direction. Every recipe owns a cone around its own direction, and the cones never overlap, so a blend falls inside at most one. That is what you're making. A blend inside no cone makes nothing and cannot be accepted. (Until draft 7 it made Murk, a junk potion; with every set of essences now a recipe, a blend outside every cone is simply between two of them, and the honest answer is "neither".)

Craft — built

Brewing, in two steps rebuilt

Brewing is mixing and nothing else. The alembic wheel went in draft 4; temperature and method went in draft 7. What goes in the pot is the whole decision.

What changed, and why

Draft 4 replaced the wheel with a temperature you held and a method you chose, Stirred or Simmered, so that a brew was graded on more than its ingredients. It worked, and it grew the wrong way. The method split the book into twins told apart only by method or by an essence window, until it held 196 recipes that made sense only with the method attached; and once a band was known, holding the temperature was a chore rather than a decision.

Draft 7 takes both out. With the ingredients rebuilt on one scale, the ratio carries the craft on its own: hitting it is arithmetic on known numbers, thrown out by drying. The Lagged cauldron and the Codex's Deft Hands only acted on heat, so they went too, and are paid back to old saves.

The Alchemy screen is two steps on one page, then bottling once the brew is done:

Step 1 — Ingredients

Tap or drag them into the pot. Essence totals and the capacity meter update as you go. Nothing is spent yet.

Step 2 — Outcome

What you would make right now, with its grade and potency. Accept spends the ingredients and starts the brew timer; Pour it back returns every ingredient unchanged. A blend inside no recipe's cone offers nothing to accept.

Then — Bottle

When the timer finishes, press Bottle it. There is nothing to choose. See Bottling.

31 recipes, and why their cones cannot overlap

There is one recipe for every non-empty set of the five essences, always in equal measure: five single-essence potions, ten pairs, ten triples, five fours and the one with all five. recipes.json holds only which essences a recipe uses, its base value, whether it is known from the start, and which painted bottle it borrows. The target direction and the cone are worked out from the set when the book loads (buildRecipes in src/sim/config.ts), because both follow from it and a hand-typed copy could only ever disagree.

Each cone is half the angle to its nearest neighbouring recipe. Two cones that each reach halfway can touch but never overlap, so no blend has two answers and there is nothing to rank. The cost is that the crowded parts of the space get narrow cones:

Grade and potency are separate, on purpose

So a small pot on the ratio is an S, and a Sovereign off it is an F. The two axes ask different things of the player — precision and scale. An earlier balance pass tied the top grades to potency after a single ingredient graded S; the 31-potion book undoes that on purpose. One clean ingredient is exactly on its potion's ratio, a small perfect potion is still perfect, and its size is what keeps its price down.

Why rejecting has to be free

Ingredients stay yours until you press Accept. That single rule is what makes hunting for a ratio fair — nobody experiments when trying costs ingredients. It is also why the pot keeps its contents across a reload: losing a half-built blend to a tab switch would make experimenting expensive, which is exactly what it must not be.

Then it brews

Accepting starts a timer scaled to potency — 30 seconds for a Minor, 45 minutes for a Sovereign — and the brew finishes whether you are watching or not. When it is ready, bottling is one press on the same page. The old separate Workbench screen is gone; bottling was never a different activity.

What this cost, and what it bought

  • Lost: the temperature hunt, which was the one part of discovery that was a search, and a second axis a recipe could be told apart on. The book fell from 196 recipes to 31.
  • Gained: a potion is its ratio and its size, and nothing else. Every blend has at most one answer, every value on the screen says what it is, and the difficulty sits in arithmetic on a known scale rather than in a gauge.
  • Kept: the craft. A three-essence ratio in a pot of mixed-freshness ingredients is not trivial, and a narrow cone rewards getting it exactly right.

Discovery rebuilt

The five single-essence potions are known from the start. The other 26 are in the book only as a hint — no name, no ratio — until a pot comes out as one and is accepted. The hint is placeholder text for now: it says how many essences the recipe calls for, and nothing more.

Only accepting teaches. A rejected pot tells you nothing, because free rejection has to stay strictly worse than committing, or the free option would be the only option. The knowledge is per-save rather than per-character — it is the player's, not the alchemist's — which is why Remembered Recipes in the Codex is worth buying: it carries your most-brewed discoveries through a retirement. It does not count the five starting recipes, because every new shop knows those already.

Draft 5's hidden temperature band, where a recipe you had not made kept its temperature from you, went with heat.

Tuning found by playing it

S first needed 96 purity. With purity falling linearly to zero at a cone's edge, that left almost no room on the narrow three- to five-essence cones once drying had moved the ratio at all. S now starts at 90, and the other bands moved down by the same six points, so the letters stay evenly spaced. The heat-drift tuning this heading used to record went with heat.

Then the bigger mixes went back up. With one ladder for every potion, a five-essence blend was as easy to take to S as a single-essence one, and the difference between them was only in the price. S stays at 90 for one or two essences, and is from 94 for three and 97 for four or five — the last above the 96 that started this — with every band above F rising by the same amount. The scale is grading.sFromByEssenceCount. Whether 97 leaves room on a ±13° cone once drying has moved the ratio is one of the questions under Status.

Finish

Bottling rebuilt

One press. A finished brew becomes a potion you can sell, supply or deliver, and there is nothing to choose on the way.

When a brew's timer finishes, press Bottle it — on the bench's Ready card, on the pot itself, or in the station — and it is bottled. No dialog, no choice, nothing consumed. The bottle carries the potion, its grade and its potency, and its value is the recipe's base value × the potency multiplier × the grade factor. Every potency can be bottled, Sovereign included.

Every bottle counts the same way: one unit toward a contract, one to a shelf slot, and to a hero exactly the grade it was brewed at.

What changed, and why

Bottling used to be two more choices: a vessel, from a waxed pouch to a crystal orb, which capped potency and multiplied value, and a seal, from a cork to the Ashwalker's mark, which added appeal, contract pay, a haggle ceiling or a grade for heroes. Draft 8 took both out. They were not part of the design the game is being built to now, and playing it showed what they cost: most vessels and seals were sold by no merchant at all — the crystal orb, the only vessel that would hold a Sovereign brew, among them — and the supply of glass bottlenecked the whole economy. The vessel moulds and the sealing press, which were meant to make them, never did anything and went too.

Old saves had the vessel and seal stripped from every bottle and the bottle priced again, were paid back for the moulds and the press, and got back the extra pouches from a stacked shelf as bottles of their own. Draft 7 had already removed the third choice, the bottle form — tincture, salve, powder and the rest — for asking questions of a brew that fought the rule that a potion is its ratio and its size.

Bottled potions never degrade. Freshness lives on raw ingredients only. Once it's in a bottle it's inventory forever — Pillar 04, and it means you can stockpile before a big contract without the game punishing the planning.

Market

Two of the three ways to sell

Passive and planned. The third gets its own section because it became a whole minigame.

A · The shelf — automatic

Stock display shelves and set a price per item as a percentage of fair value. Customers arrive on a tick and buy probabilistically:

P(sale) = footfall × appeal × demand(item) × priceCurve(ask / fairValue)

Footfall comes from renown, shop décor, district, weekday and time of day. Appeal starts equal for every bottle and is raised by the shelf's board and the furnishings on show. Demand drifts slowly per item type, so what sells changes week to week. The price curve is steep: at 130% of fair value sales nearly stop, at 80% they fly. Setting prices is the whole game here, and it's one slider per shelf slot.

Offline, footfall runs at 40%. Being present matters; leaving doesn't hurt.

A shelf slot holds one bottle. Putting several out fills free shelves in order, one to each, so how many is the player's decision and which shelf is not.

Furnishings new

The shop has five spots — wall, window, counter, nook, floor — and nineteen furnishings to fill them, so a complete collection still leaves you choosing which five are out. A piece does nothing while it sits in the back room; placement is what pays, which is the entire difference between this and an equipment list.

The set was rebuilt around what the painted art actually is, rather than forcing the art onto the names it was designed with. Seven banners compete for the wall, each a step up in footfall and in price. The window is a genuine side-grade: a potted fern sells by day, a lit urn sells after dark. And the nook trades passive appeal against the haggle ceiling — a cut diamond makes people browse, a skull chalice makes them stop arguing.

B · Contracts — planned

A board of three standing requests from guilds, barracks, temples and noble houses. Each names a faction, a potion, a minimum grade and a quantity, with a deadline and a payout — nothing about how it is bottled.

CONTRACT Greycloak Barracks reputation: cordial Deliver 8 × Aqua–Terra Potion · grade B or better Deadline 8 days (in-game · paused while away) Payout 1,240g · +40 renown · unlocks Barracks tier 2

Contracts are the main gold faucet and the reason to plan a supply chain. Failing one costs the payout and some faction reputation — nothing else. You can also partially deliver for a prorated payout and no reputation hit: a mercy valve that keeps a bad week from feeling bad.

Every bottle is one unit: an order for nine potions takes nine bottles. Most of the board is commissioned from each faction's palate, among the potions you have discovered; the rest comes from nine hand-written orders across four factions, and a faction may never outrank the contracts it posts. The board never shows the same order twice at once — one faction, the same potion, grade and quantity — though orders that are merely close still appear.

Market — the minigame

The haggle reworked

Rock-paper-scissors, played against a customer whose stance you can read but who changes it the moment you land a counter.

Named customers walk in with a want, a hidden budget, and a stance — telegraphed through their portrait, their opening line, and their district. Your pitch actions form the other half of a 3×3: each one counters exactly one stance and backfires against exactly one other.

Three hidden values move: their interest, their patience, and their ceiling — the most they'll pay.

  • Neutral — small interest gain, normal patience cost. Safe, slow.

No stance interaction at all. Costs patience heavily, and converts accumulated interest directly into ceiling. It's the cash-out: worthless early, devastating once you've stacked two counters. Knowing when to stop pitching and close is the skill ceiling of the whole system.

Three rounds, then you name a final price. At or under their ceiling, it sells. Patience hitting zero ends the haggle at their last standing offer — you never walk away with nothing, and the worst outcome is a shelf-price sale. An Appraise upgrade, bought from Vessa, reveals their next stance instead of just their current one.

Five buttons and a price stepper. Touch and mouse identical, no dragging, no timing.

Reading the stance new

Pose carries the stance; dialogue carries the personality. Each customer has three portrait poses, and the pose alone is enough to identify the stance — arms crossed and turned slightly away for Sceptical, chin raised and squared-on for Haughty, glancing off-frame with weight on one foot for Impatient. The dialogue line is flavour and character, never the only tell.

Two reasons this is worth the art budget. It keeps the minigame readable without reading, which matters for accessibility and matters more for localisation — a stance inferred from an English idiom breaks the moment it's translated. And it makes customers feel like people reacting to you rather than a state machine with a label, which is the whole appeal of the face-to-face channel.

Interface

Input & screen model

Mouse-only on desktop, touch plus buttons on mobile, one layout serving both.

The rule that makes this work

Every drag has a tap equivalent, and every hover has a tap equivalent. Drag an ingredient into the cauldron, or tap it and press Add — both ship on both platforms, as you asked. We build the tap path first and treat drag and hover as accelerators, so nothing is ever unreachable on either platform and we never write two UIs.

Desktop · mouse only

  • Left click everything; right click never required
  • Hover tooltips on all items and stats
  • Wheel scrolls lists and zooms the garden, cave and shaft
  • Drag-and-drop for cauldron, shelves and cave placement
  • Keyboard shortcuts exist but are strictly optional

Mobile · touch + buttons

  • Portrait-first; landscape supported, never required
  • 44px minimum targets, bottom-anchored action bar in thumb reach
  • Both tap-drag and tap-select → tap-action, everywhere
  • No multi-touch gestures, no long-press-only actions
  • System back = up one screen, always

Screens as built

Eight, on a persistent bottom bar (mobile) or left dock (desktop): Shop (shelves, inventory, walk-ins and haggling) · Board (contracts) · Market (merchants) · Grounds (garden, cave and shaft as sub-tabs) · Cauldron (all alchemy, including bottling) · Roster (heroes, missions, favour) · Ledger (statistics and a paginated event log) · Settings (text size, saves, the Mastery Codex).

Two things moved from the plan. Merchants earned a screen rather than an overlay — with four traders on different cycles and the upgrade trees to browse, a banner was not enough surface. And the Workbench folded into the Cauldron, because bottling was never a separate activity from the brew it finishes — and it is now a single press.

Eight does not fit a phone at a comfortable tap size, so the bar scrolls rather than squeezing every label into four characters. Each item keeps its full 44px target.

Layout strategy

One responsive layout at three breakpoints — phone portrait, tablet, desktop — differing in how many panels are visible at once, never in what's available. On desktop the cauldron screen shows inventory, cauldron and result side by side; on phone those are three tabs of one screen.

The five essences are the game's most colour-dependent system, so each one carries a glyph as well as a hue — triangle, droplet, square, chevron, crescent — and the glyph appears everywhere the colour does: chips, ingredient badges, cave beds, potion tints, recipe cards. A player who can't distinguish Ignis from Terra can always distinguish ▲ from ■.

The palette itself is chosen for luminance separation as well as hue, so the five read as five distinct greys under deuteranopia and protanopia. Before content lock the shipping palette gets run through a simulator and corrected — the values in this document are the starting point.

  • Minimum 4.5:1 text contrast, 3:1 for UI boundaries, in both themes
  • No information conveyed by colour alone, anywhere
  • prefers-reduced-motion respected — animations and transitions in the panels are cut to nothing, and no rule in the game depends on watching one
  • A high-contrast essence palette as an explicit setting, for players who want more separation than the default

Text scaling

All panel UI is DOM (see architecture), sized in rem, and honours the device's system text size. An in-game scale setting covers 85%–150% on top of that. Layouts are tested at 150% with the longest supported language — nothing may clip, truncate or overlap. This is the single strongest practical argument for DOM panels over canvas text.

Localisation from day one

  • Every user-facing string lives in a keyed table (src/i18n/en.json). No string literals in game code — enforced by a lint rule from the first commit.
  • Keys are semantic (garden.plot.emptyAction), never English text used as a key.
  • Numbers, dates, durations and currency go through Intl, never hand-formatted.
  • Plurals and gendered grammar use ICU message format, so no string is assembled by concatenation.
  • Layouts assume +40% string length for German and Finnish; nothing is sized to fit English exactly.
  • RTL isn't in scope now, but the layout uses logical CSS properties (margin-inline-start) throughout so it stays possible.
  • English ships first. The infrastructure means adding a language later is a translation contract, not an engineering project.
Presentation

Art direction & the pipeline

Painted 2D, sourced from a mix of AI generation and asset packs — which makes a normalisation pass the most important thing in this section.

Direction

Warm shop interior lit by glass and coals; cool world outside; the cave lit only by what you put there. The palette this document uses is the game's: aged verdigris and bottle-green as structure, amber and lamplight as highlight, muted pigment tones for the five essences. Hand-lettered labels, ledger paper, brass fittings. Cozy but not saccharine — a working shop, not a cottage.

What the pipeline actually became rebuilt

The plan above was a style bible: recolour everything into one ramp, relight it from one direction, and the collage becomes a game. What arrived was six hundred sprites already painted in a consistent hand, so the recolouring pass was never needed. The problem turned out to be the opposite one — not making the art agree with itself, but making the game agree with the art.

Colour decided essence, until draft 7

For draft 6 the load-bearing idea was that each sprite's own pixels chose its essence. Hue picked the essence, chroma weighted the vote so a grey stem could not outvote a scarlet cap, and the amount came from a tier decision rather than from the art. Two corrections were needed on the way: brown first read as fire, putting 58 of 144 herbs in Ignis, and pale wispy things read as shadow until they were moved to air. The picture also chose the source — a mushroom for the cave, a gem for the shaft, a monster's bone for a hero's pack.

It gave every sprite an honest direction, and it was the wrong answer for the amounts. A tier decision spread strengths over a continuous range, so the set grew to 385 ingredients, most of them near-duplicates at strengths nobody had chosen, and balancing a ratio became a matter of luck. Draft 7 kept the pictures and dropped the derivation.

Now: slots on a scale, and the pictures fill them

scripts/gen-ingredients.js declares the slots — each category, each set of essences, each amount on the 4-to-48 scale — and derives everything that follows from them: one crop per herb, one cave species per fungus, a stratum every five metres, the expedition loot tables and each merchant's trade goods. ingredients.json records which id, and so which name and which picture, fills each slot. A slot nothing fills is reported rather than invented, and a data test fails if the data and the generator disagree, so a hand edit that breaks a rule is caught instead of being silently undone the next time the script runs.

The ingredients that were kept reuse their ids, so their names and art carried over and nothing was drawn or renamed. Which picture fills which slot is now a choice rather than a measurement, so the UI's rule still stands: a picture that reads as fiery on an Umbra ingredient fights every label that calls it Umbra.

Potions name their bottle

Each of the 31 recipes names the painted bottle it borrows in its art field. The script that used to match bottles to recipes by the colour of the liquid did it by renaming files to recipe ids and deleting the rest; with 31 recipes and nearly two hundred paintings it would have destroyed most of them, so it went.

The tools that remain

The scripts that turned sprites into ingredients — art-rename, art-essence, art-minerals, art-match, art-adopt and art-ingest — were removed in draft 7, along with art-potions, gen-recipes and art-recipes, which would each have overwritten the new book. What is left is general tooling: art-build (normalises the art and writes the manifest), the atlas packer, dekey, effects, shelves, survey, upright, the contact sheet, dedupe, the name filler art-i18n, the two data generators gen-heroes and gen-ingredients, and ui-check.

Source art goes in art/ at whatever size it arrives; a build step trims each image to its own opaque bounding box, fits it into the target with a small margin, and centres it — so a tall feather and a squat mushroom occupy the same cell and a grid of them reads evenly.

Loading: measured, then decided

Three attempts, and the numbers settled it:

  • Preload everything. Phaser held the scene back until the last of six hundred requests landed — the world sat empty for 34 seconds.
  • An atlas. Named as the real fix and built, then measured: 2.8 MB fetched whole before anything can draw, against 135 KB for the sixteen sprites the busiest screen shows. An atlas wins when a screen draws most of a set at once; the garden showed sixteen crops of the three hundred there were then. Not shipped. The packer is kept for the day an encyclopedia or a recipe book needs it.
  • Lazy, with a prefetch. Load the two dozen sprites every scene needs, then fetch the rest on demand — and ask for a screen's sprites when the screen is chosen rather than discovering them mid-draw, which is what caused art to pop in one item at a time. 612 ms to first paint, and a screen change costs a single request.

Day/night is not four painted backgrounds per room — that's 4× the art budget. It's one painted background plus a lighting layer: a tinted overlay, a warm/cool grade, and swapped light-source sprites. Painted once, lit four ways.

Placeholders, for the gaps that remain

Not grey boxes. Generated from the game's own data — an ingredient's badge is tinted by its dominant essence and stamped with that essence's glyph, which is also the colour-blind affordance. The plot and shelf are still drawn rather than painted: the plot as tilled soil with a raised lip, alternating furrows and deterministic grit; the shelf as a board with a lit front edge, a cast shadow and brackets, so bottles stand on it rather than floating over a stripe.

Badges are now generated only where there is no art. Generating one for every ingredient quietly claimed the texture key, so when the real sprite arrived Phaser found the name taken and dropped it — painted art never appeared in the world no matter how correctly it loaded. That bug cost an hour and is the reason this paragraph exists.

Flagging, not lecturing

Two practical notes on mixed sourcing: the App Store and Play Store are both fine with AI-generated art today, but some asset-pack licences forbid redistribution in a form where art can be extracted — which a web build makes trivially easy. Worth checking licences against a web release specifically, since that's our primary platform. I'd also keep a simple provenance column in the asset manifest (generated / pack / commissioned) so this is answerable later without an archaeology dig.

Engineering

Architecture

Phaser 4.2.1 and TypeScript, with the whole simulation kept out of Phaser entirely. Confirmed in draft 2.

The one structural decision

A headless simulation core in plain TypeScript — no Phaser import anywhere inside it. It owns world state, the clock, and every rule in this document. The Phaser layer renders that state and sends it intents. Three payoffs:

  • Offline progress is the same code as online progress. sim.advanceTo(now) runs on resume and on every frame. No second implementation to drift.
  • The rules are unit-testable without a renderer. Given this cauldron, assert this grade. Given 72 hours away, assert this gold.
  • The engine is replaceable. If Phaser turns out wrong for the UI-heavy screens, the game survives.

Phaser for scenes, DOM for panels

Confirmed. The world renders in Phaser — garden, cave, shaft, shop interior, characters, day/night lighting. The panels are plain DOM overlaid on the canvas — inventory lists, the brewing station, contract board, ledger, equipment shop, tooltips, settings. Management games are 60% text and lists, and DOM gives us text layout, scrolling, screen readers, dynamic type and responsive breakpoints for free; each of those would be a week of work in canvas. It's also what makes the 150% text-scaling commitment realistic. The two halves communicate through one intent bus.

Saving

One versioned JSON blob with a migration chain, written on every meaningful action and on visibilitychange. Web uses localStorage; mobile uses Capacitor Preferences. Local only — no cloud sync. Autosave rotates through three slots so a corrupt write can't end a 40-hour game, and manual export/import of the save as a file ships from M1, because local-only saves make that the player's only backup.

Debug tooling is a first-class feature new

The headless sim core is what makes the debug time panel nearly free: since sim.advanceTo(t) is the only way time ever moves, "skip four days" and "simulate 72 hours away" are the same one-line call the resume path already makes. The panel is a DOM overlay talking to the same intent bus as the game UI, gated behind import.meta.env.DEV and a hidden gesture in release builds, and tree-shaken out of production.

Every duration, cost and rate in this document lives in data/ as a config value, never a constant in code — so day length, crop timers and price curves are all tunable at runtime and diffable in git.

Audio

Out of scope. A silent audio bus stub sits behind every event that would eventually make a sound (audio.play('cauldron.bubble') resolving to a no-op) so adding sound later is a content task, not a refactor. Costs nothing now.

Shipping

Vite builds the web bundle; Capacitor wraps it for iOS and Android; desktop is the same web build in a browser. No Steam, so no achievements API, no cloud saves, no controller support, no launcher — which keeps the whole platform layer to one adapter file. Vitest covers the sim core; the UI gets smoke tests only.

369 tests, all green (one skipped), in a few seconds. The simulation core is covered end to end — offline catch-up, save migration, every economy rule — while the UI gets smoke coverage only. That split is deliberate and has caught real bugs: a freshness tile that spent the wrong batch, a text-scale setting that flipped the desktop layout, a rank that could disagree with its own renown.

The balance harness once briefly took fourteen seconds of that, because the blend search ran 314 ingredients against 71 recipes. It was spending its time on overhead rather than arithmetic: allocating a fresh object per candidate and recomputing magnitudes inside the hottest loop. With the pool prepared once into flat typed arrays, incremental updates, and results memoised by target, the same search takes a fortieth of the time and returns the same answers.

Two suites are not correctness tests at all. The balance harness takes measurements — seeds per planting, gold per hour, how far a blend can get from a recipe — and fails when a number drifts outside a range we have decided is sane; when one goes red the right response is usually to look at the number, not the assertion. The coverage suite asks the flat question this project kept failing to ask: is this field read by anything at all?

Status

What the building taught

Five milestones and two overhauls in. These are the places the code disagreed with the design, and the questions that only playing it could raise.

Where the code took a different road

  • The alembic wheel is gone, and so is what replaced it. Beautiful on paper, opaque in the hand; then temperature and method, which were legible but split the book into twins. Brewing is mixing alone. See Brewing for what that cost.
  • Merchants got a screen; the Workbench lost one. Four traders and the upgrade trees need browsing; bottling never needed its own room, and is one press now.
  • Nothing is scheduled that can be derived. Merchant visits and stock, cave spread, walk-in customers — all pure functions of the day counter. This is the single decision that has paid off most: offline catch-up needed no special cases anywhere.
  • Décor had to be placed, not owned. As equipment it was a shopping list with no decision in it. Five spots and nineteen pieces is a decision.
  • The art decided the data — and then it stopped. Deriving essence from a sprite's colour gave every picture an honest direction, but left the amounts on a continuous range, and a ratio built from arbitrary strengths is luck. The data now decides: a slot on one scale, filled by a picture.

Two claims this document made that measurement overturned

  • "A texture atlas is the real fix." Built, measured, discarded: 2.8 MB against 135 KB for what a screen actually shows. Right technique, wrong access pattern. Kept for the day a screen draws the whole set.
  • "Four-essence blends all overlap." Excluded from the recipe space on that reasoning; the overlap check turned out to enforce it far better than the rule of thumb, rejecting the ones that genuinely collide and keeping seventeen that do not. The 31-potion book goes further: all five equal-measure fours are recipes, with cones of about 13°.

Both were reasonable guesses. Neither survived a number, which is the argument for the harness existing at all.

The lesson that cost the most

Five separate times, a data file declared something and the far end was never wired: five pieces of equipment, half the Mastery Codex, five vessel and seal fields, the soil on every crop, and seven of the eight town modifiers. A sixth kind was worse — ten upgrades that worked perfectly and no merchant sold. Draft 8 found the reverse of it: the vessel moulds and the sealing press were on sale for 900g and 1200g and did nothing at all, because the crafting they unlocked was never built.

Every one of them passed a full green suite, because the tests measured behaviour that was working. None asked whether the thing could be reached at all. Three habits came out of it, and they are the most valuable thing in this document:

  • Test the far end, not the near one. Buy the item, then assert that something the game reads actually moved. It is the only check a disconnected field cannot pass.
  • Ask the flat question mechanically. A coverage test now fails when any field in any data file is read by nothing in src/. Exemptions need a written reason, and the exemption list is itself checked.
  • Reachable is not the same as declared. A recipe needs a blend that lands inside its cone, not an ingredient pointing vaguely at it. An upgrade needs a seller. An ore needs a depth you can actually dig to.

Balance questions the tests can't answer

  • Nobody has played long enough to know the curve. The Rank X target of ~40 hours is still a guess, and Mastery's payout has never been tested across a real second run.
  • The towns have never been played, only wired. Cinderhold's 40% crop penalty against 135% ore is a guess at what makes a run feel different rather than just worse.
  • Contracts may dominate the shelf. They pay better by design, but nothing yet measures by how much.
  • Thirty-one recipes is complete; nobody knows if it is enough. One per set of essences exhausts the equal-measure recipes, and a thirty-second would need unequal ratios and narrower cones. Whether 31 potions with placeholder names are interesting is the open question, and only playing it answers it.
  • The narrow cones may be too narrow. S moved from 96 purity to 90 because drying left no room on the three- to five-essence cones, then back up to 94 and 97 on exactly those cones, because a five-essence S had become as easy as a single-essence one. Whether ±13° with S at 97 is a satisfying target for a five-essence potion or a frustrating one is untested.
  • 151 ingredients may still be many to choose between. Every one is gatherable and sits on one scale, which makes them easier to reason about than the three hundred before. Whether the cauldron needs a filter and a favourites list is untested.

The risk that was unguarded

For five milestones this repository had no version control. A generator crashed mid-write and truncated heroes.json to zero bytes — the roster, the favour bands, four biome loot tables — and there was nothing to restore from. It was rebuilt by hand from what happened to be recoverable, and every generator now writes through a temporary file and renames, which is atomic.

That fixed the specific failure and none of the general one. The repository is now under git, which is the actual answer, and the generators that remain are committed with it.

Still to decide

  • What the potions are called. The 31 are named for their essences, priced by how many they use, and hinted only by that count, and each borrows a bottle painted for some older recipe. Names, prices and hints are the largest content job left.
  • Recipe scrolls. Discovery works by brewing and observing, so there is no way to buy your way toward a recipe you cannot stumble into. Merchants were always meant to sell them; they don't yet.
  • The art that is still missing. No painted plot or shelf, so the world view draws those. Four ingredients — Featherfern, Windwort, Gloamroot and Gloomcap — have no painting yet and draw their essence badge. Customers have no portraits, which the haggle was designed around — three stance poses each is the largest remaining art ask in the game.
  • How long a session should be. Everything is built for a ten-minute check-in, but the day length, brew timers and mission durations have never been tuned against someone actually playing that way.

The Capacitor scaffold is in place; the APK is waiting on a JDK and an Android SDK rather than on any code. Alongside that, the placeholder potions want real names and prices.
